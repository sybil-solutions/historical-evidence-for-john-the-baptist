# 1. What evidence actually survives?

## No document written by John survives

We have no authenticated letter, diary, sermon manuscript, official arrest warrant, contemporary portrait or securely identified first-century inscription naming the historical Baptist. The surviving accounts were written after his death. That is normal for many ancient figures, but it limits what a responsible biography can contain. John the Baptist must also be distinguished from John son of Zebedee, the John traditionally associated with the Fourth Gospel, and the John addressed in the Apocryphon of John.

The evidence is mainly literary. Historians assess its age, sources, purposes, independence, internal coherence and fit with the ancient setting. A text can preserve historical memory while also developing theology. Conversely, multiple copies of a story do not become multiple independent witnesses.

## The early source map

| Source | Approximate composition | Main contribution and limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Mark | Around 65-75 CE | Earliest surviving continuous Gospel narrative: preaching, baptism of Jesus, followers and execution. Already a theological account. |
| Matthew | Often around 80-90 CE | More teaching, Jesus's praise and John's hesitation to baptize Jesus. Uses Mark and other tradition. |
| Luke and Acts | Commonly late first century; dates disputed | Only canonical infancy story, distinctive ethical instruction and later reception of John's baptism. Two volumes by one author. |
| Josephus, Antiquities | 93/94 CE | Jewish, non-Christian account of John's morality, baptism, following, imprisonment and execution. Does not connect him with Jesus. |
| Gospel of John | Often the 90s CE, with stages possible | John as witness; overlapping ministries and transferred disciples. Does not narrate the baptism itself. |
| Later Christian and Mandaean works | Diverse, frequently much later | Essential for reception history; possible older traditions must be argued individually. |
| Qur'an | Seventh century CE | Yahya's miraculous birth, virtues and prophetic status; no narrative of Jesus's baptism. |

These are conventional working dates, not dates printed on original manuscripts. Earlier and later datings are defended. The usual chronological framework and Markan dependence

are explained in the scholarly introductions to the Gospels. [S03] [S04] [S05] [S06]

Matthew and Luke share material often assigned to a hypothetical sayings source called Q. No manuscript of Q survives, and other explanations of their relationship exist. Q is a scholarly reconstruction, not another excavated book. Paul's undisputed letters, earlier than the written Gospels, do not explicitly discuss the Baptist. Silence in occasional letters neither supplies a biography nor disproves John's existence.

## Why Josephus is especially valuable

Josephus describes John approvingly as a teacher of justice toward other people and devotion toward God. He explains that moral purification should precede bodily washing, then says Antipas feared the potential of John's influence over the crowds. The account locates John's imprisonment and death at Machaerus. It also reports that some Jews interpreted Antipas's subsequent military defeat as divine punishment. Josephus does not affirm that interpretation as a demonstrated supernatural cause.

This testimony is widely treated as authentic, although a minority disputes its authorship or transmission. Dorota Hartman's philological study finds it compatible with Josephus's style and context. Rivka Nir has argued for possible Christian intervention; her 2026 Oxford Handbook chapter surveys the dispute without a decisive verdict. The responsible position is strong evidential use with an acknowledged textual debate. [S07] [S08]
