# Christian worship, art and liturgy

John's Christian afterlife is the largest of any figure in this archive except
Jesus. It is not evidence about the first century; it is evidence about what
communities did with him, and it is unusually well documented in institutions
that can be checked.

## The calendar

Two feasts carry him: the Nativity of John the Baptist on 24 June (Luke 1
supplies the date six months before Christmas), and the Beheading on 29 August.
The Byzantine and Latin calendars, the Orthodox synaxaria and the Roman
martyrology all transmit them, and the 24 June feast is old enough that Augustine
preaches on its relation to the solstice.

## The images

John is one of the most painted figures in Christian art, in three recurring
types: the forerunner who points at Christ (the *Deesis*), the ascetic in camel
hair with a reed cross, and the severed head on a platter. The Met's painted copy
of the Deesis mosaic from Hagia Sophia, Leonardo's *Saint John the Baptist* in
the Louvre, and Caravaggio's Beheading in Valletta's St John's Co-Cathedral are
all documented works in documented collections — useful precisely because the
provenance, not the legend, is what can be verified.

## The liturgical texts

The Orthodox synaxis and the Latin solemnity present John as the greatest of
those born of women and as the forerunner, drawing on Matthew 11:11 and Luke
1:76. The texts are institutional statements about how the churches read him;
they are not independent historical testimony, but they show the shape the
figure took in worship.
