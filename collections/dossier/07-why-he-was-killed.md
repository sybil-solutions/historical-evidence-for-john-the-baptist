# 7. Why was he killed?

## The ruler was Herod Antipas

The Herod responsible is Antipas, ruler of Galilee and Perea, not Herod the Great of the infancy stories. Mark calls him king, but his formal title was tetrarch. The Herodian family reused names and intermarried extensively, making careless summaries particularly misleading.

Three explanations survive in our main accounts:

| Account | Main motive and narrative |
|---|---|
| Josephus, Antiquities 18.116-119 | Antipas fears that John's influence could produce political unrest and acts preemptively |
| Mark 6:17-29 | John condemns the marriage; Herodias wants revenge; Antipas protects John until a banquet promise traps him |
| Matthew 14:3-12 | Antipas wants John killed but fears the crowd; the banquet provides the occasion |

Matthew depends on Mark for much of this story. Their agreement is not independent confirmation of each banquet detail. Their different portraits of Antipas also matter: fear restrains him in Matthew, while fear of John's potential influence prompts execution in Josephus. [S35] [S36]

## A moral confrontation could also be political

In the Gospels, John condemns Antipas's marriage to his brother's wife, Herodias. That is a public challenge to a ruler's conduct under Jewish law, not merely private gossip. Josephus's broader narrative connects Antipas's new marriage and repudiation of the daughter of Aretas IV with dynastic conflict. Political authority, marriage alliances and religious legitimacy belonged to the same world.

It is consequently possible that the moral and political explanations overlap. A popular teacher denouncing the ruler could be dangerous without commanding an army. But possibility is not confirmation: we cannot recover a documented meeting in which Antipas explained all his motives, nor verify the banquet's private conversations.

John's execution indicates a perceived threat in the source tradition. It does not establish that he planned a violent revolt. Josephus describes what Antipas feared the crowds might do, not an actual insurrection organized by John.

## Salome and the expanding story

The Gospel dancer is not explicitly called Salome. Josephus names Salome in his discussion of Herodias's family, but does not connect her to John's death. Identifying the dancer with her combines the accounts. Mark 6:22 also has a significant manuscript variant affecting whether the girl is described as Antipas's daughter Herodias or Herodias's daughter.

Herodias's earlier husband, called Herod by Josephus, must be distinguished from Philip the tetrarch, whom Salome later married. The label Herod Philip I is a conventional attempt to organize or harmonize this family, not the name Josephus

gives that earlier husband. The famous dance of seven veils is absent from the Gospel accounts. None of these cautions requires denying the execution; they concern additions and ambiguities surrounding it. [S37]

Mark and Matthew describe beheading; Josephus records the killing without specifying the method. The Gospels also describe John's disciples burying him. They do not provide a verifiable chain connecting that burial to the many later relic claims.

## What his death meant afterward

For Christian readers, John became a prophet who refused to exempt political power from moral judgment. His death also anticipates Jesus's: a respected religious figure is arrested, a ruler is portrayed as constrained by pressures, and followers attend to the body. Those literary connections explain the narrative's force; they are not additional independent evidence for every detail of either trial or execution.
