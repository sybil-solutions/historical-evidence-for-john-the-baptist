# The Sozopol relics: what the AMS and DNA study actually reported

In 2010, excavation near the church of Saint John the Forerunner on Sveti Ivan
island, off Sozopol in Bulgaria, produced a reliquary containing human and
animal bones and a small inscribed box naming Saint John and his feast day.

## The published study

Kostova and colleagues published the scientific analysis in the *Journal of
Archaeological Science: Reports* (29, 2020). Two results matter:

- **Radiocarbon.** One human metacarpal gave a calibrated date of 5-75 CE at
  68.2% probability. That range is compatible with the lifetime of John the
  Baptist.
- **Ancient DNA.** The samples yielded very little human DNA, and none of the
  damage patterns expected of genuinely ancient molecules. The authors concluded
  that the sequences obtained most likely came from modern contamination.

## What it establishes, and what it does not

It establishes that a relic tradition attached to John existed at that church,
that the bones are old enough not to be disqualified by their date, and that no
usable genetic profile was recovered. It does not identify the individual.

Age compatibility is the weakest possible form of identification: a date range
covering the first century fits millions of people. The same logic would
"confirm" almost any first-century bone. Reports that DNA had "proved" the
relics belonged to John inverted the study's own conclusion, which was that the
DNA evidence was uninformative and probably contamination.
