# Appendix B. How scholarship disagrees

The recurring disagreements concern how much later Christian editing can be reconstructed, whether baptism replaced sacrifice, whether John belonged to a distinct sect, and how much later literature preserves early memory. These seven entries provide complementary approaches. Publisher descriptions, bibliographic records, and accessible abstracts establish scope and selected arguments; inclusion does not imply that every listed book was read in full for this report.

1. Joan E. Taylor, The Immerser: John the Baptist within

Second Temple Judaism (Eerdmans, 1997)[S14]. A central starting point for studying John inside Jewish religious life. Read alongside her later response to Marcus, below: shared purification practices and vocabulary do not by themselves prove that John belonged to Qumran. The interpretive question is how distinctive John's movement was within a varied Jewish environment.

2. Robert L. Webb, John the Baptizer and Prophet: A

Socio-Historical Study (Sheffield Academic Press, 1991)[S90]. Organizes the evidence around John's two public roles, baptizer and prophet. Its comparisons with Jewish washings and prophetic movements help explain the social

meaning of his actions, beyond the Gospel narratives' focus on Jesus. Particularly useful for baptism's context and the movement's political implications.

3. John P. Meier, A Marginal Jew, volume II: Mentor,

Message, and Miracles (1994)[S91]. Places John prominently within reconstruction of Jesus's career. It offers an influential historical-critical baseline for their relationship. Its conclusions should be compared with later debates about memory and historical criteria; an influential reconstruction is not a surviving biography by John or Jesus.

4. Joel Marcus, John the Baptist in History and Theology

(University of South Carolina Press, 2018)[S92], paired with Federico Adinolfi and Joan Taylor, “Catching John in four nets?” (2021)[S29]. Marcus reconstructs a John whose own mission had greater centrality than Christian portrayals allow. Adinolfi and Taylor challenge his competition, Qumran, sacramental, and messianic proposals, favoring a teacher-successor relationship. Read together to avoid presenting either strong rivalry or John's messianic self-identification as settled.

5. James F. McGrath, John of History, Baptist of Faith:

The Quest for the Historical Baptizer (Eerdmans, 2024)[S20]; also Christmaker: A Life of John the Baptist (2024)[S93]. The substantial study gives unusual weight to Mandaean materials and uses Jesus's teaching to reconstruct his mentor; Christmaker offers an accessible presentation. The crucial methodological issue is how securely later traditions and a pupil's teachings reveal the earlier teacher. Treat specific reconstructions as arguments requiring assessment.

6. Thomas Kazen, “John's Immersions: Ritual

Purification, but from What?” (2024)[S21]. The accessible abstract argues against understanding immersion as a

substitute for sacrifice, using theories of conceptual metaphor and ritual. This offers a concrete counterpoint to replacement theories: acknowledging a distinctive repentance ritual does not establish that John intended to abolish or displace the Temple's sacrificial system. The full article was not accessed for this annotation.

7. Roberto Martinez, “Contemporary Research and

Debated Issues on John the Baptist” (2024)[S94]. A recent guide to debates over message, Elijah, Jesus, baptism, Josephus, and Qumran. Its abstract and bibliography were consulted; the full paywalled article was not accessed. It is useful for identifying the questions still under discussion and locating further scholarship, rather than supplying a claim that those questions have been resolved.
