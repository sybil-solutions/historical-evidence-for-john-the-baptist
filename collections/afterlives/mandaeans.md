# The Mandaeans

The Mandaeans are the only surviving religious community that treats John — in
their own language, Yuhana — as a central figure on his own terms rather than as
a forerunner to Jesus. They are also the hardest case in this archive to use as
historical evidence, because the community's literature is late, its language is
eastern Aramaic, and its relationship to first-century discipleship is precisely
what is in dispute.

## Who they are

An ethno-religious community historically of southern Iraq and Khuzestan in
southwestern Iran, with a substantial diaspora since the 2003 war. Their
scriptures include the *Ginza Rabba* (the Great Treasure), the *Book of John*
(*Drasha d-Yahya*), and liturgical and magical texts. John appears as a priest
and teacher within a cosmogony of light and darkness, saviours and the ascent of
the soul. He is not the founder of the religion in its own account, and
describing Mandaeans as Christians who preferred John misrepresents a separate
religion.

## What their practice shows

Baptism is central and repeated: the *maṣbuta*, performed in flowing water
called *yardna* — Jordan — with priestly actions around the immersion. That a
living community has baptized in running water in John's name for centuries is
real evidence of a durable tradition. It is not evidence of an unbroken
institutional line back to the Jordan Valley.

## Why the lineage question stays open

Rudolf Macuch, Jorunn Jacobsen Buckley and Şinasi Gündüz have defended versions
of a Palestinian origin and eastward migration; other scholars stress the
Mesopotamian character of the language and the community, and the difficulty of
documenting any link to John's own disciples. Edmondo Lupieri's account is
useful precisely because it separates the attraction of an origin story from the
evidence needed to establish it. The Book of John's own portrayals of John,
Jesus and Miriai are better evidence for the encounters of a later community
with Jewish, Christian and Islamic traditions than for the first century.
